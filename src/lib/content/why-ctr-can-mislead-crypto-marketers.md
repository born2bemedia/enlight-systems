<p>Click-through rate is one of the first numbers marketers check when evaluating a campaign. A higher CTR usually suggests that an advertisement is attracting attention, while a low CTR can indicate weak creative, poor targeting or an unsuitable placement.</p>

<p>The problem starts when CTR becomes the measure of campaign success rather than one signal within a larger acquisition funnel. For crypto marketers, this distinction matters because a click can be several steps away from a valuable user. A prospect may click an advertisement, visit a landing page, register an account, complete KYC, connect a wallet, make a deposit or perform an on-chain transaction. Each additional step changes the economic value of that original click.</p>

<p>Recent crypto advertising benchmarks illustrate the problem. HypeLab's 2026 analysis of more than 200 publishers reports display CTRs of roughly 0.08% to 0.25%, while native placements can reach 0.20% to 0.65%. The same research reports post-click conversion rates of 2% to 4% for wallet-aware campaigns. CTR therefore varies substantially by format and audience, while the business outcome depends on what happens after the click.</p>

<p>Campaign B generates twice as many clicks and costs half as much per click. On a standard advertising report, it can look significantly better. It produces half as many acquisitions. The reason is simple: traffic quality matters more than traffic volume once the user leaves the advertisement.</p>

<p>This distinction becomes particularly important in crypto, where conversion often requires additional actions. HypeLab's 2026 benchmarks put click-to-wallet-connection rates for DeFi campaigns at roughly 3% to 8%, followed by another conversion step from wallet connection to the first transaction. A campaign optimised for clicks can therefore produce thousands of inexpensive visitors while generating relatively few users who reach the action that actually matters to the project.</p>

<h2 id="interest-not-value">CTR Measures Interest, Not Economic Value</h2>

<p>CTR answers one question: How many people clicked after seeing the advertisement? It does not answer:</p>

<ul>
  <li>How qualified were those users?</li>
  <li>How many completed registration?</li>
  <li>How many connected a wallet?</li>
  <li>How many deposited funds or completed a transaction?</li>
  <li>How much revenue did the acquired users generate?</li>
  <li>What did each acquired user actually cost?</li>
</ul>

<p>This is why CTR should be connected to downstream metrics rather than evaluated independently. A useful performance chain for crypto campaigns looks like this:</p>

<div class="post-chain">
  <span class="post-chain__item">CTR</span>
  <span class="post-chain__arrow">→</span>
  <span class="post-chain__item">CPC</span>
  <span class="post-chain__arrow">→</span>
  <span class="post-chain__item">landing-page conversion</span>
  <span class="post-chain__arrow">→</span>
  <span class="post-chain__item">activation</span>
  <span class="post-chain__arrow">→</span>
  <span class="post-chain__item">CAC</span>
  <span class="post-chain__arrow">→</span>
  <span class="post-chain__item">revenue or user value</span>
</div>

<p>Each stage changes the interpretation of the previous one. For example, a high CTR combined with a poor landing-page conversion rate can indicate that the advertisement promises something the landing page does not deliver. A strong landing-page conversion rate followed by weak activation may point to onboarding friction. A healthy acquisition rate combined with poor retention can indicate that targeting is bringing users who are unlikely to become long-term customers. Looking only at CTR hides these relationships.</p>

<h2 id="connects-clicks">The Metric That Connects Clicks With Money</h2>

<p>Cost per acquisition gives marketers a more useful economic perspective because it connects advertising spend with a defined business outcome. The calculation is straightforward:</p>

<div class="post-callout">CAC = Total acquisition spend ÷ Number of acquired customers</div>

<p>The difficulty in crypto is defining the acquisition event correctly. For an exchange, a signup may be insufficient if the commercial objective is a funded account. For a DeFi protocol, a wallet connection may have limited value if the user never completes a transaction. For a wallet provider, installation may matter less than successful wallet creation and subsequent activity. The correct conversion event therefore depends on the business model.</p>

<h2 id="what-to-do">What Should Marketers Do With CTR?</h2>

<h2 id="real-question">The Real Question Behind CTR</h2>

<p>The useful question is not "Which campaign has the highest CTR?" It is:</p>

<div class="post-callout">"Which campaign turns marketing spend into the most valuable users?"</div>

<p>That requires marketers to follow the journey beyond the click and connect advertising performance with conversion, activation and acquisition costs. A campaign with a 0.5% CTR can outperform one with a 2% CTR if its visitors convert at a much higher rate and generate greater customer value.</p>

<p>For crypto projects, where the journey from advertisement to meaningful user activity can involve several additional steps, this distinction can determine whether a marketing budget is being scaled intelligently or simply buying more clicks.</p>

<p>Enlight provides the unified performance view needed to make that distinction. Instead of judging campaigns from isolated platform metrics, teams can monitor the relationship between acquisition activity and downstream performance, identify where conversion efficiency is being lost, and allocate budget according to actual marketing outcomes.</p>

<p>CTR tells you who clicked. Your funnel tells you whether those clicks were worth paying for.</p>
